# 03 Class Flow and Spaced Repetition

Back to [[00 Greekquent Index]] · [[01 Lesson Scripts]]

## The 60-minute class

| Block | Min | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Spaced review | 10 | Cards from earlier lessons (auto-picked, see below), plus last week's red words |
| Warm-up script | 15 | The short script: read it, then perform it |
| Main scene | 15 | The longer scene: read it in pairs, then perform it without the paper |
| Fork it | 12 | Replay the main scene with swaps, twists, and the generic forks |
| Exit ticket | 5 | Recall 3: each student says 2 new chunks without looking |

Each lesson has three recall points: at the end of the warm-up, after the fork rounds, and the exit ticket. Each one is papers face-down, English cue, Greek answer, with the gesture. Why this order works, with the research: [[04 How Memory Works]].

New words come in through the warm-up and the main scene, never as a bare list. Use the black marker for those on the packet.

## The review ring

Every lesson reviews four earlier lessons, at **1, 2, 4, and 8 weeks back**. The gaps double, which is the expanding-interval pattern behind spaced repetition: each time you meet a phrase just as it's starting to fade, it stays with you longer. Each lesson note's "Spaced review" table pulls 2 chunks from each of those lessons. It rotates which chunks it pulls, so a phrase from Lesson 3 shows up in Lessons 4, 5, 7, and 11, and each time you see a different slice of that lesson.

| Lesson | Reviews |
|---|---|
| 5 | L4 · L3 · L1 |
| 9 | L8 · L7 · L5 · L1 |
| 12 | L11 · L10 · L8 · L4 |
| 17 | L16 · L15 · L13 · L9 |
| 26 | L25 · L24 · L22 · L18 |

By the end of the year, every chunk has come back about four times without anyone having to plan it. With the three recalls in its own lesson, that makes about seven recalls per phrase, spread over two months. That's the "3 + 3" rule from memory research, with one extra for good measure: see [[04 How Memory Works]].

## Colors as a paper flashcard box

The red, yellow, and green markers already sort words into boxes. Use them as a schedule (a paper version of the Leitner box system):

- **Red** (don't have it): it comes back **next week**, in the review block.
- **Yellow** (kind of have it): it comes back **in 2 weeks**.
- **Green** (have it): check it again at **4 weeks**, then at **8 weeks**. If it's still green, it's retired.
- If a word slips, it goes back to red, and its clock restarts.

The student marks the packet. You only ask "What's red this week?" at the start of class. That's the whole system, and you don't need an app for it.

## Paper in every lesson

The vault is for planning. In class, everything happens on paper and on the table. Every lesson uses the same five paper moves:

| When | Paper move | Where it is in the notes |
|---|---|---|
| Spaced review | Students re-mark words on their **printed word list**: red, yellow, or green | Review box |
| Start of the warm-up | Students **black-mark** today's new words on the list (the note gives each word's number on the list) | Top of the warm-up box |
| Fork it | **Tile round**: build the day's sentences from cut-out word tiles on the sentence mat, then flip, negate, and swap | "Every lesson" list in Fork it |
| Exit ticket | Recall 3 is **written by hand on an index card**, then said aloud. Students keep the cards, and the red ones come back next week | Exit ticket box |
| Homework | One **out in the world** mission, most of them ending in something written by hand | Homework box |

The printed word list is the year's map. Students get it on day one and mark it all year: black when a word is assigned, then red, yellow, or green at each review. By May, the list shows them how far they've come.

**Tiles and the sentence mat.** Each lesson has three sentences from its main scene cut into word tiles, plus extra tiles (δεν, ;, ., and words from the first swap slot). Verbs are shaded, so students can see that the verb sits at the center of the sentence and that δεν always goes right before it. The mat has six zones (Who · δεν · Verb · What or whom · Where, when, or how · end mark). The "Who" zone is often empty, because the verb ending already says who: Έχω means "I have". Greek word order is more flexible than English, so the mat shows the usual order, not the only one. The mat is in the textbook. The tiles are a separate file to print on card stock (Handouts/Greekquent Year One Sentence Tiles.pdf). For printing the book, use Handouts/Greekquent Year One Textbook - Print Edition.pdf (black and white, mirrored margins, 112 pages) with the separate color cover.

The textbook has a chapter for each lesson, with handwriting lines for copying and for writing from memory. In Lessons 1–6 it also has letter-writing rows.

## Optional free app: Anki

For students who want to practice between classes, the vault includes **Handouts/Greekquent Year One Anki Deck.csv**: 458 cards, each with Greek, English, and a lesson tag (L01–L26, chunk or scene). Anki is free on desktop, Android, and the web (AnkiWeb). The iPhone app is paid, but the web version works on iPhone for free. To import, choose File → Import, then pick the CSV. Students can filter the deck to the lessons covered so far by tag (for example, `tag:L0*`). Anki schedules the reviews itself. Tell students to say the answer out loud before they flip the card, and to press Again until they get each card right once that day.

## The 95% rule

A scene is comfortable when students already know about 95% of its words, roughly one new word in twenty. Each lesson note shows its figure and lists the new words, and the index has a Familiar column.

- **New words go in the warm-up.** Teach and act them out there, so the main scene has almost nothing new.
- **Length comes from old phrases.** Main scenes bring back phrases from the lessons in the review ring, which lengthens the scene and does the review at the same time.
- **Novelty comes from the situation.** A new place, a problem, a role swap, or a shift in person or time all feel new without adding vocabulary.
- **Some words are cheap.** Names, look-alike words (μουσική, ταξί), and things you can point to barely count against the 95%.
- **Fork one thing at a time.** A replay that swaps in three new words at once has stopped being practice and become new teaching.

Quick check in class: if students can say what the scene is about after one read-through, it's close to 95%.

## How to fork a scene

Forking keeps the same skeleton and changes one thing, so the scene feels new while the chunks repeat. Every Main scene has:

1. **Slot swaps**: lists of substitutes (who, what, where, when). Change one slot per replay.
2. **Two twists**: a complication that forces new lines.
3. **Generic forks**, which work on any lesson:
   - **Role swap**: A and B trade parts.
   - **Person shift** (from Lesson 9): retell the scene about him, her, or us.
   - **Time shift**: as tomorrow from Lesson 17 (θα…), and as yesterday from Lesson 22 (ήθελα, πήγα…).
   - **Speed round**: double speed, no paper.
   - **Retell round** (Nation's 4/3/2, shortened for beginners): each student retells the scene to a new partner three times, with less time each time.
   - **Mix-in**: one line from today's review cards goes into a replay.

A good pattern is three replays: the first straight, the second with one slot swap, and the third as a retell round.

## Beginners and intermediate students in one vault

Every lesson has an **Intermediate** note (in Scripts/Intermediate) on the same theme, with a longer scene, open conversation questions, and "Push it further" tasks. In a mixed class:

- Everyone does the spaced review together. Intermediate students answer in 2 sentences with a reason or a time.
- Beginners work on the warm-up and the main scene, while intermediate students run the longer scene in parallel.
- Everyone comes back together for the fork rounds. The intermediate students play the "complication" roles.

Over the year, the intermediate track covers the same subjects as the beginner track, so a returning student gets a refresher on familiar ground, just with longer and harder language.
