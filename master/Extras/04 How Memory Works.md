# 04 How Memory Works

Back to [[00 Greekquent Index]] · [[01 Lesson Scripts]]

The lesson notes stay short. This note explains why they're built the way they are. Each move below already sits inside every lesson, at the step where it happens.

| Move | Where it is in every lesson |
|---|---|
| Count recalls, not repetitions | Recall 1, 2, 3 in the lesson, then the review ring at 1, 2, 4, and 8 lessons back |
| Come back to it after a gap | Recall 2 comes after the main scene and fork rounds, not right after teaching |
| Say it, don't pick it | Review cards, recall points, and Anki: English cue, Greek answer out loud |
| Guess first | The first 30 seconds of the warm-up |
| Mix it up | A "Mix-in" line from the review cards in the fork rounds |
| Act it out | The gesture table, folded inside each warm-up |
| Same story, less time | The retell round, the last replay in Fork it |
| Write it by hand | Exit-ticket index cards, and the textbook's writing lines |
| Move the words | The tile round in Fork it |
| Keep the four strands balanced | The 60-minute flow, plus homework |

## Count recalls, not repetitions

There is no magic number of repetitions. A 2019 meta-analysis of 26 studies found a medium effect of repetition on vocabulary learning, and the effect depended on spacing, picture support, and how engaged learners were ([Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa, 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/lang.12343)). The number of encounters needed ranged from about 2 to 20 ([summary of that meta-analysis](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/108688/8/13621688221140521.pdf)).

A more useful target is successful recalls. Rawson and Dunlosky recommend recalling an item correctly 3 times when first learning it, then relearning it 3 more times at widely spaced intervals ([Rawson & Dunlosky, 2011](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ934616)). This course gives each phrase 3 recalls in its own lesson and about 4 more in the review ring, so about 7 recalls over two months.

## Come back to it after a gap

Recalling something you've already got right still pays off. In one study, repeated recall of items students already knew improved retention by more than 100% compared with dropping them from practice, while re-reading them added nothing ([Karpicke & Roediger, 2007](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2007/2007_Karpicke_Roediger_JML.pdf)).

Gaps between sessions matter even more than gaps within one class. Students who recalled items once in each of three spaced sessions remembered more than twice as much as students who recalled them three times in one session ([Association for Psychological Science summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/teaching-current-directions-studying-psychopathology)). That's why the review ring at the start of each class is the backbone, and the in-class recalls are the support.

## Say it, don't pick it

After a week, students who had been tested on a passage recalled 61% of it, against 40% for students who had re-read it four times ([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2006/2006_Roediger_Karpicke_PsychSci.pdf)). Producing an answer helps more than recognizing it among choices ([review summary](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zGfjkW1ociSmSUCLcpbhKjf)). So every check in this course is an English cue with a spoken Greek answer, papers face-down.

## Guess first

Asking learners to guess an answer before they're taught it improves later memory for that answer, even when the guess is wrong ([review of pretesting research](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5)). In class, take two or three guesses, praise any that are close, and then teach the phrase. Keep it to 30 seconds.

## Mix it up

Mixing different kinds of practice, instead of doing one kind at a time, has a moderate benefit on average ([meta-analysis, Brunmair & Richter, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)). In Fork it, one line from the day's review cards goes into a replay, so old and new phrases share the same scene.

## Act it out

Words learned with gestures were recalled better at every check, including after 14 months ([Macedonia, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4260465/)). Two things matter: the gesture has to match the meaning, and students have to make it themselves, because watching the teacher do it isn't enough ([same review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4260465/)).

Greek caution: never push an open palm with spread fingers toward someone. That's the μούτζα, the most traditional Greek insult ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountza)). The OK ring made with thumb and forefinger is also obscene in Greece ([Cultural Atlas](https://theculturalatlas.org/greek-culture/greek-culture-communication)). The gestures in these notes wave with the fingers together, keep "open hand" gestures palm-up, and count with the palm facing yourself.

## Same story, less time

In Paul Nation's 4/3/2 activity, a learner tells the same story to three listeners in turn, in 4, then 3, then 2 minutes. Studies found gains in fluency, and also in grammatical accuracy and complexity ([Nation, 2007](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2007-Four-strands.pdf)). Nation suggests shorter times for beginners ([Nation](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/foreign-language_1125.pdf)), so the retell round here uses 60/45/30 seconds in Lessons 1–8, 90/60/45 seconds in Lessons 9–16, and 2 minutes/90 seconds/1 minute from Lesson 17.

## Write it by hand

Handwriting helps most with learning a new alphabet. Adults learning Arabic letters by writing them by hand learned faster than those who typed them or watched videos, and they were better at reading and spelling new words ([Wiley & Rapp, 2021, Johns Hopkins summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/07/210708111508.htm)). That's why the textbook has letter-writing rows in Lessons 1–6.

For vocabulary in general, the evidence is mixed. An EEG study found more brain connectivity during handwriting than typing ([van der Weel & van der Meer, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1219945/full)), but critics point out that the study measured no actual learning ([commentary](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1517235/full)). So writing by hand here always comes paired with recall: students write from memory, not just copy.

## Move the words with your hands

Sentence tiles haven't been tested the way the moves above have, so treat them as a way to do recall and production with your hands. Building a sentence from an English cue is production, not recognition. Flipping, negating, and swapping tiles is the same slot-swap forking the class does out loud, done on the table. The tiles also make word order visible: the shaded verb tile anchors the sentence, and δεν always goes right before it.

## Keep the four strands balanced

Nation's four strands are input (listening and reading), output (speaking and writing), deliberate study, and fluency. He suggests about a quarter of the time for each, counting study outside class ([Nation, 1996](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/1996-Four-strands.pdf)).

A rough tally for one class:

| Block | Min | Mostly |
|---|---|---|
| Spaced review | 10 | Fluency (known phrases, at speed) |
| Warm-up | 15 | Deliberate study (new phrases, boards, gestures) |
| Main scene | 15 | Input (first read-through) and output (performing) |
| Fork it | 12 | Output (replays) and fluency (retell round) |
| Exit ticket | 5 | Deliberate study |

That's roughly 20 minutes of study, 10 of input, 15 of output, and 15 of fluency. Input is the thin strand in class. Two cheap fixes: read the main scene aloud once while students just listen before they read it themselves, and lean on the homework (re-reading the story daily, the watch-list), which Nation counts toward input.

## At home

- **Anki:** say the answer out loud before flipping the card, and press Again until each card is right once that day. Anki then spaces the next review itself.
- **Paper packet:** the red, yellow, and green markers already work as a spaced schedule. Review red words every day, yellow every few days, green now and then.
